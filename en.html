Summary

Key findings of the study

Central messageImproving the living conditions of trans women, sex workers and migrant women exposed to trafficking requires action on three fronts at once: laws and policies, community-based organisations and public debate. On the legal and policy front, rights are being rolled back and gaps persist between formal guarantees and effective access. On the community front, organisations are the main capacity in place to respond to the needs of these populations, but their continuity is at risk owing to a lack of resources. In public debate, stigmatising voices prevail, while those of the communities themselves remain sidelined. The result is visible in everyday life, where the very institutions meant to protect their rights also perpetrate violence against them.

How to read the study

A pathway of access to rights

The study follows the pathway of each profile of women in all their diversity, from their social position to the impact on their lives.

This comprehensive baseline study addresses the CRECE Project's target population: trans women, women sex workers and migrant women exposed to trafficking. Lesbian and bisexual women were included as an analytical comparison group to identify shared and specific barriers to accessing rights, from an intercategorical and intersectional approach, without being a target population of the project.

1Social position and contextIdentity, nationality, work, territory
2Discrimination, exclusion and violenceWhere it happens and who perpetrates it
3Networks and protective factorsWho she turns to and what she does
4Access to rightsHealth, justice, protection, participation
5Impact on her lifeWhich in turn shapes her social position

The pathway repeats itself: what happens at each stage strengthens or weakens the next.

Policy and legal framework

A broad, fragmented framework under backlash

102laws, policies and rulings reviewed
93in force as of September 2026
7backlash milestones between 2016 and 2026

The backlash began on the streets in 2016 and reached the school curriculum in 2026. Only the courts managed to reverse measures.

None of these measures explicitly repeals a right: they hollow it out.

International obligations are not effectively incorporated into national or local legal frameworks. Moreover, the State provides fragmented responses to the different populations.

Conclusions and recommendations

What the baseline shows

1

The backlash has already happened

Seven milestones between 2016 and 2026. Protecting existing gains is a result in itself.

2

The gap lies between the law and access

93 laws in force, fragmented and centred on HIV.

3

Institutional violence is the most consistent finding

62.4% of trans women sex workers were stopped by the police.

4

Sex work is the common thread, but the harm varies

Police stops: 57.9% among those engaged in sex work, compared with 1.5% among those who are not.

5

Having insurance is not the same as having access

PrEP use of 2–3% in Peruvian profiles; up to 61.5% of migrants uninsured.

6

Violence goes unreported because the institution is a risk

Between 59% and 78% did nothing after experiencing violence.

7

Organisations sustain protection without funding

12,781 services a year and 33 of 49 representatives without any incentive.

8

Community voices are missing from the media

Almost no article quotes a community-based organisation.

9

The local level is the most viable route

The only proposal implemented was municipal.

10

Trans women are invisible in official statistics

Trans femicides are recorded as homicides of men.

Recommendations

Recognise the agency of people and their organisationsDo not make services conditional on leaving sex work or on migration statusDo not shift the State's responsibilities onto organisations

Protect existing gains

  • Defend the protective laws in force.
  • Take part in redesigning the gender equality policy.
  • Strengthen alliances for strategic litigation.

End institutional violence

  • Comply with the reparations in the Azul Rojas Marín case.
  • A protocol for police and municipal security guards that prohibits extortion.
  • A complaints channel accompanied by community organisations.

Prioritise the local level

  • Lima: municipal security practices by district and access to health care.
  • Callao: continuity of care pathways across jurisdictions.
  • Tumbes: referral pathways that distinguish sex work, coercion and trafficking.

Strengthen organisations

  • Support tailored to each type.
  • Training in financial management, project design and data.
  • Voluntary formalisation and sustainable leadership.

Health beyond HIV

  • Scale up PrEP and continuity of treatment.
  • Integrate mental, sexual and reproductive health.
  • Access to public health insurance for victims, regardless of migration status.

Amplify community voices

  • Train spokespersons from the organisations.
  • Campaigns that dismantle reframing.
  • Also train the police and the Ministry of the Interior.

Approaches of the study

Intersectionality

The main analytical framework. It examines how gender identity, sexual orientation, nationality, occupation and territory interact to produce distinct inequalities.

Human rights

Guides the analysis and research ethics: do no harm, informed consent, confidentiality and respect for self-identification.

Gender equality and diversity

Recognises gender as a system of power and the sexual and gender diversity of participants.

Backlash against gender equality

The UNRISD and UN Women framework on organised backlash against gender equality.
